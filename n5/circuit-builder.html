    1. The potential divider

    Two resistors share the supply

    Two resistors in series across a supply make a potential divider. The 6.0 V of the supply is shared between them: V1 across R1 and V2 across R2.

    Right now both have a resistance of 10 kΩ, so they get equal shares.

    Change one resistor

    R2 is a variable resistor, so you can change its resistance.

    1.R2 is made bigger. What happens to V2, the voltage across R2?

    The bigger resistance always gets the bigger share of the voltage. Move the slider and watch V1 and V2.

    Look at V1 + V2: whatever you do, it is always 6.0 V.

    The rule

    Bigger resistance, bigger share of the voltage.

    The shares always add up to the supply voltage: Vs = V1 + V2.

    On your relationships sheet: V2 = (R2 / (R1 + R2)) × Vs

    2.A potential divider has R1 = 2.0 kΩ and R2 = 4.0 kΩ on a 6.0 V supply. Calculate V2.

    V

    That's V1, the share across R1. R2 is the bigger resistor, so it gets the bigger share.

    The answer can't be more than the supply voltage. Divide R2 by the total resistance R1 + R2.

    Hint

    The total resistance is R1 + R2. What fraction of it is R2? R2 gets that fraction of the 6.0 V.

    V2 = (R2 / (R1 + R2)) × Vs = (4.0 / (2.0 + 4.0)) × 6.0 = 4.0 V. (The kΩ cancel, so you can leave them as they are.)

    The middle point

    In every switching circuit, a wire goes from the middle point of a potential divider to a transistor. The bottom of the divider is joined to 0 V.

    So the voltage at the middle point (compared with 0 V) is the same as the voltage across the bottom part, V2. Change the bottom part's share, and you change the voltage the transistor gets.

    3.R1 and R2 are both 10 kΩ, on a 6.0 V supply. What is the voltage at the middle point?

    Equal resistors get equal shares, so each has 3.0 V across it. The middle point is 3.0 V above the 0 V line.

    2. The LDR

    A resistor that senses light

    An LDR (light-dependent resistor) is a resistor whose resistance depends on how much light falls on it. The ohmmeter is measuring its resistance.

    1.The light gets brighter. What happens to the resistance of the LDR?

    The brighter the light, the lower the resistance of an LDR. Slide from Dark to Very bright and watch the dot move along the graph.

    An LDR in a potential divider

    Now the LDR is in a potential divider with a 10 kΩ resistor, on a 5.0 V supply.

    2.It gets darker. What happens to the voltage across the LDR?

    In the dark the LDR's resistance goes up, so it takes a bigger share of the 5.0 V. Slide the light level and watch the voltage across it.

    What to remember

    Brighter: lower resistance, smaller share of the voltage.

    Darker: higher resistance, bigger share of the voltage.

    3.Which of these would use an LDR? Tap all that apply.

    The street light and the phone screen both need to sense how bright it is, so they use an LDR.

    3. The thermistor

    A resistor that senses temperature

    A thermistor is a resistor whose resistance changes with temperature. The ohmmeter is measuring its resistance.

    1.The thermistor gets hotter. What happens to its resistance?

    The hotter it gets, the lower the resistance of the thermistor. Slide the temperature and watch the dot move along the graph.

    Careful: an ordinary metal wire is the other way round. Its resistance goes up as it gets hotter.

    A thermistor in a potential divider

    Now the thermistor is in a potential divider with a 1.0 kΩ resistor, on a 5.0 V supply.

    2.It gets colder. What happens to the voltage across the thermistor?

    Colder means a higher resistance, so the thermistor takes a bigger share of the 5.0 V. Slide the temperature and watch.

    What to remember

    Hotter: lower resistance, smaller share of the voltage.

    Colder: higher resistance, bigger share of the voltage.

    3.Which of these would use a thermistor? Tap all that apply.

    The fridge and the thermostat both need to sense temperature, so they use a thermistor.

    4. Transistors as switches

    Meet the circuit

    A transistor is a switch with no moving parts. Instead of a finger pressing it, it is switched on by a voltage.

    This one is an npn transistor. Its three connections are the base, the collector and the emitter. Tap each of the ringed parts to find out what it does.

    Switching it on

    An npn transistor switches on when the voltage across its base and emitter reaches 0.7 V. Then current can flow through it from the collector to the emitter, and the LED lights.

    1.The voltage across the base and emitter is 0.9 V. Is the LED on or off?

    0.9 V is more than 0.7 V, so the transistor is switched on and the LED lights. Now make the variable resistor bigger: its share of the voltage goes up. Watch for the moment the LED comes on.

    The MOSFET

    An n-channel enhancement MOSFET does the same job. Its connections are the gate, the drain and the source. This one switches on when the voltage across its gate and source reaches 2.0 V. (Exam questions tell you the switching voltage of the MOSFET they use.)

    2.The voltage across the gate and source is 1.5 V. Is the LED on or off?

    1.5 V is less than 2.0 V, so the MOSFET is still off. Use the slider to find where it switches on.

    Matching connections

    3.Match each npn transistor connection to the MOSFET connection that does the same job.

    Drag each one onto its partner, or tap one and then its partner.

    The base and the gate are where the switching voltage goes in. Current flows from the collector to the emitter, or from the drain to the source.

    The key idea

    A transistor only responds to the voltage across the part joined between its base and emitter (for a MOSFET, its gate and source).

    In exam circuits, that is the part at the bottom of the potential divider, joined to the 0 V line.

    4.What is this component?

    It is an n-channel enhancement MOSFET: the gate is separate from the channel, which is drawn in three parts, with the arrow pointing in.

    5. The relay

    Two separate circuits

    A relay is a switch worked by an electromagnet. Its coil is in one circuit and its switch is in another.

    On the left is a 5.0 V circuit with a switch and the relay coil. On the right is a 230 V mains circuit with the relay switch and a heater. No wire joins the two. Tap each of the ringed parts.

    Working the relay

    1.The switch is closed, so current flows in the relay coil. What happens to the heater?

    The current makes the coil an electromagnet, which pulls the relay switch closed. That completes the mains circuit. Close the switch (here or on the circuit) and watch.

    Look at the moving dots. In the 5.0 V circuit they go one way (d.c.). In the mains circuit they keep changing direction (a.c.).

    Why use a relay?

    2.Why do switching circuits use a relay to turn on a mains heater?

    The transistor circuit can only give a small current at a low voltage. The relay lets that small current switch on a separate circuit with a big current or a high voltage, and keeps the mains separate from the electronics.

    What it does

    This circuit switches an LED on automatically when it gets dark, like a street light. Tap each of the ringed parts to find out what it does.

    Which voltage matters?

    1.The transistor switches on when the voltage across which part reaches 0.7 V?

    The LDR is joined between the base and the emitter (the 0 V line), so the voltage across the LDR is the voltage across the base and emitter.

    Make it dark

    2.It gets darker. What happens to the resistance of the LDR?

    In the dark the LDR's resistance goes up. Now slide the light level down slowly and watch each link of the chain.

    Choosing the resistor

    The resistor at the top decides how dark it has to get before the LED comes on.

    3.The LED should come on earlier in the evening, while it is still fairly bright. Should the resistor at the top be bigger or smaller?

    Hint

    The LDR needs 0.7 V across it. Which gives the LDR a bigger share: a bigger or a smaller resistor above it?

    A smaller resistor at the top gives the LDR a bigger share, so it reaches 0.7 V while its resistance is still fairly low (while it is still fairly bright). That's why exam circuits often use a variable resistor here: to set the light level at which the circuit switches.

    7. Fan comes on when it gets hot

    What it does

    This circuit switches on a fan (a motor) when it gets hot. It uses a MOSFET as the switch. Tap each of the ringed parts.

    Look carefully: the thermistor is at the top of the potential divider this time.

    Which voltage matters?

    1.The MOSFET switches on when the voltage across which part reaches 2.0 V?

    The resistor is joined between the gate and the source (the 0 V line), so the MOSFET responds to the voltage across the resistor. Many pupils pick the thermistor here, because it's the part that senses the temperature.

    Turn up the heat

    2.It gets hotter. What happens to the voltage across the resistor?

    Hint

    First think about the thermistor: what happens to its resistance, and so to its share of the voltage? The two shares always add up to 5.0 V.

    Hotter: the thermistor's resistance decreases, so its share of the 5.0 V goes down and the resistor's share goes up. Slide the temperature up slowly and watch the chain.

    Swap them over

    3.The thermistor and the resistor are swapped over, so the thermistor is at the bottom. Now the fan would come on when it gets…

    With the thermistor at the bottom, the MOSFET responds to the voltage across the thermistor. That goes up when the thermistor's resistance goes up: when it gets cold. Swapping the two parts of a potential divider swaps what the circuit does.

    9. Alarm sounds when it gets light

    What it does

    This alarm sits inside a jewellery box. When someone opens the lid, light gets in and the alarm sounds.

    Compare it with walkthrough 6: the LDR is now at the top of the potential divider. Tap each of the ringed parts.

    Which voltage matters?

    1.The transistor switches on when the voltage across which part reaches 0.7 V?

    The resistor is joined between the base and the emitter (the 0 V line), so the transistor responds to the voltage across the resistor.

    Open the lid

    2.The lid opens and light gets in. What happens to the voltage across the resistor?

    In the light the LDR's resistance decreases, so its share goes down and the resistor's share goes up. Slide the light level up slowly and watch the chain.

    The rule for any switching circuit

    1. Find the part between the base (or gate) and the 0 V line.

    2. The output comes on when the voltage across that part goes up to the switching voltage.

    3. That happens when its own resistance goes up, or when the resistance of the part above it goes down.

    3.A switching circuit has a resistor at the top of its potential divider and a thermistor at the bottom. When does its output come on?

    The thermistor is between the base (or gate) and 0 V. When it gets cold its resistance goes up, so the voltage across it goes up, and the transistor switches on.

    10. Time delay

    What it does

    When you close the switch, the LED comes on a few seconds later. The potential divider is a resistor and a capacitor. Tap each of the ringed parts.

    Close the switch

    1.The switch is closed. What happens to the voltage across the capacitor?

    The capacitor charges up through the resistor, so the voltage across it rises slowly. Close the switch and watch: when the voltage reaches 0.7 V, the transistor switches on.

    Opening the switch doesn't empty the capacitor. Press "Empty the capacitor" before you try again.

    Changing the delay

    2.The resistor is changed for one with a bigger resistance. What happens to the delay?

    A bigger resistance means a smaller charging current, so the capacitor takes longer to reach 0.7 V. A bigger capacitor would also give a longer delay. Try two different resistors and compare the times.

    Write the explanation

    3.Explain why the LED comes on a short time after the switch is closed. Put the sentences in order. Two of them are wrong: put those in "Not needed".

    Drag each sentence to its place, or tap a sentence and then its place.

    When the switch is closed, the capacitor starts to charge, so the voltage across the capacitor rises slowly. When it reaches 0.7 V the transistor switches on and the LED lights.

    Build your own

    Tasks

    1.Join two 10 Ω resistors in series. Use an ohmmeter to measure their total resistance.

    Hint

    An ohmmeter is used with no cell or battery in the circuit. Join one end of the ohmmeter to each end of the pair of resistors.

    In series the resistances add: RT = R1 + R2 = 10 + 10 = 20 Ω. Adding resistors in series always increases the total resistance.

    2.Now join two 10 Ω resistors in parallel and measure their total resistance. Is it more or less than one resistor on its own?

    Hint

    Parallel means each resistor has its own branch. Both ends of each resistor are joined to the ohmmeter.

    1/RT = 1/R1 + 1/R2 = 1/10 + 1/10, so RT = 5.0 Ω. Adding a resistor in parallel gives the current another path, so the total resistance goes down: it is less than the smallest resistor.

    3.Build a circuit with a battery, a fuse and a lamp in series. Then add a wire across the lamp to make a short circuit. What happens to the fuse?

    Hint

    Join the extra wire from one end of the lamp to the other end, so the current can go round the lamp instead of through it.

    The short circuit has almost no resistance, so the current becomes much bigger than the fuse rating. The thin wire in the fuse melts and breaks the circuit before the cables can overheat. The fuse protects the wiring, not the person.

    4.Build a potential divider: a 6 V battery with a 10 Ω resistor and a 20 Ω resistor in series. Work out the voltage across the 20 Ω resistor, then check it with a voltmeter.

    Hint

    Tap a resistor to choose its value. The bigger resistor gets the bigger share of the supply voltage.

    V2 = (R2 / (R1 + R2)) × Vs = (20 / (10 + 20)) × 6 = 4.0 V. The 10 Ω resistor gets the other 2.0 V, and 4.0 + 2.0 = 6.0 V, the supply voltage.

    5.Build a potential divider from a battery, a resistor and an LDR, with a voltmeter across the LDR. Then slide the Light level down to Dark. What happens to the voltmeter reading?

    Hint

    The Light slider appears once there is an LDR on the grid. Try a 1 kΩ resistor.

    The reading goes up. As it gets darker the LDR's resistance increases, so it takes a bigger share of the supply voltage.

    6.Build a circuit that switches an LED on automatically when it gets dark. Use an LDR, a resistor, an npn transistor and an LED.

    Hint

    The LDR and resistor make a potential divider; join the base to the point between them. The transistor switches on when the voltage across the base and emitter reaches 0.7 V. Put the LED, with a resistor to protect it, between the positive side and the collector. If the LED stays on in the light, use a bigger resistor in the divider.

    The LDR goes between the base and the emitter. In the dark its resistance increases, so the voltage across it rises. Once it reaches 0.7 V the transistor switches on, and current flows through the LED.

    7.Build a circuit that switches on a fan (a motor) when it gets hot. Use a thermistor, a resistor, a MOSFET and a motor. Test it with the Temperature slider.

    Hint

    When the thermistor gets hot its resistance goes down, so the other part of the divider gets more of the voltage. This MOSFET switches on when the voltage across its gate and source reaches 2.0 V.

    The thermistor goes at the top of the divider and the resistor between the gate and the source. As the temperature rises the thermistor's resistance falls, so the voltage across the resistor rises. At 2.0 V the MOSFET switches on and the motor runs.

    Challenge8.Build a circuit in which an LED comes on a few seconds after you close a switch. You will need a capacitor, a resistor and a transistor.
